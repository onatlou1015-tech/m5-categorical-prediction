K‑Nearest Neighbors K‑NN

⏱️ 100 minutes · classify · predict · evaluate
🎯 Understand K‑NN & categorical prediction 📊 Use distance to classify new data ✅ Find the best K value
CATEGORY · PREDICTION
🏷️ Categorical output Predict a label, not a number. e.g., “Attack” vs “Defense”, “Fire” vs “No fire”.
📐 Distance matters Euclidean distance measures how “close” two data points are in feature space.
👥 K nearest neighbours The new point takes the majority class of its K closest neighbours.
⚖️ Choose K wisely Small K = sensitive to noise; large K = smoother but may underfit.

🔍 Core idea “birds of a feather”

K‑NN is built on a simple intuition: similar things are close to each other. By measuring the distance between data points, we can look at the K nearest neighbours and see what category they belong to — then assign that category to the new point.

⭐ The K‑NN algorithm in 6 steps:
1. Choose K (number of neighbours).
2. Calculate the distance from the new point to every point in the training data.
3. Sort distances from smallest to largest.
4. Pick the K closest neighbours.
5. Count how many of those neighbours are in each category.
6. Assign the category with the majority vote.
📐 Euclidean distance (between point A and point B):
d = √( (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² )

🏷️ What is categorical data?

Categorical data (also called qualitative data) represents categories or groups rather than numerical values. It answers "what kind?" or "which group?" — not "how much?" or "how many?"

📌 Two types of categorical data:
  • Nominal: Categories with no natural order (e.g., colours, animal types, countries)
  • Ordinal: Categories with a meaningful order (e.g., "small", "medium", "large"; "elementary", "high school", "university")

📋 Activities learn by doing

8.1 “My life is similar to someone else’s” – radar graphs 15 min

  • In groups of 8, define 8 indicators (e.g., love beach, reading, gaming, etc.). Rate each from 1–5.
  • Create a radar graph in Google Sheets (Insert → Chart → Radar). Use row 1 as headers, column A as labels.
  • Compare graphs — whose life is most similar to yours? This builds intuition for “closeness” in multi‑dimensions.

📊 How to read a radar graph

  • Each axis represents one indicator (e.g., “Likes beach”, “Likes gaming”).
  • Each polygon represents one person. The further the point is from the centre, the higher their rating (1 = centre, 5 = outer edge).
  • Similar shapes mean similar preferences. If two polygons overlap closely, those two people have very similar lifestyles.
  • Area of the polygon gives a rough sense of overall “intensity” – a larger area means higher ratings across many indicators.
💡 Key insight: In K‑NN, we use distance instead of visual overlap, but the idea is the same — closer = more similar.
❓ Guiding questions for your group
1. Which two students have the most similar radar shapes? Why do you think that is?
2. Which student has the largest overall area? What does that tell you about their lifestyle?
3. If you had to place a new student (with ratings: beach=4, movies=3, food=5, mountains=2, gaming=4, sports=3, reading=5, photo=4) — who would they be most similar to?
4. How does the radar graph help you understand “multi‑dimensional similarity” better than just comparing one indicator at a time?
5. If you were to calculate the Euclidean distance between two people using their 8 ratings, what would that distance represent?

8.2 “Which group is better?” – game characters 30 min

  • You are a game designer: classify Dog Lock and Pork with gravy as “Attack” or “Defense”.
  • Use the dataset of 10 characters (damage/sec, health, group).
  • Calculate Euclidean distance from the new character to all existing ones.
  • Apply K‑NN (try K = 1, 3, or 5) to assign a group.

📐 How to use the distance formula – step by step

We want to classify Dog Lock (Damage = 36, Health = 165).

1
Write down the formula:
d = √( (dmg₂ − dmg₁)² + (hp₂ − hp₁)² )
2
Pick a character to compare with. Let’s use Rewind (Damage = 30, Health = 105).
Dog Lock (36, 165) vs Rewind (30, 105)
3
Plug the numbers into the formula:
d = √( (30 − 36)² + (105 − 165)² )
4
Calculate the differences & square them:
(30 − 36) = −6 → (−6)² = 36
(105 − 165) = −60 → (−60)² = 3,600
5
Add the squares & take the square root:
36 + 3,600 = 3,636
√3,636 ≈ 60.3
6
Interpret the result:
The distance between Dog Lock and Rewind is 60.3.
💡 Smaller distance = more similar (closer in the feature space).
📋 Now repeat this for ALL 10 characters. Then sort the distances from smallest to largest to find the K closest neighbours!
❓ Questions to guide your analysis
1. Which character is closest to Dog Lock? What group do they belong to?
2. For K=3, what are the groups of the 3 nearest neighbours? What is the majority vote?
3. For K=5, does the classification change? Why might that happen?
4. If Dog Lock has very similar stats to both Attack and Defense characters, what does that tell you about the decision boundary?
5. Repeat the process for Pork with gravy (Damage=50, Health=145). Is the classification the same or different? Why?

  • Predict wildfire risk for temperature = 18°C, humidity = 44% using the 8‑day dataset.

  • Test your K value (3, 5, or 7) on 5 test cases. Compare with other groups to find the most accurate K.

1

Temperature = 18°C, Humidity = 44%
2
Calculate the distance from the new point to EACH of the 8 training days using:

3
Record your distances in the distance column of the table.
4
Sort the data by distance from smallest to largest (use Excel/Sheets: Sort & Filter → Filter → Sort Smallest to Largest).

Repeat for K=5 and K=7. Record your results in the table.
7
Test your chosen K value on the 5 test cases. Compare your predictions with the actual results to calculate accuracy.

🤔 Where does the K come in?

You've probably noticed the radar graph and distance calculations don't mention K at all. So where does it fit?

💡 Think of it like this:
  • Radar graph (Activity 8.1) = “Who looks similar?” — visual intuition
  • Distance calculation (Activity 8.2) = “How similar are they exactly?” — numbers
  • K (Activity 8.3) = “How many of the most similar people should we ask before deciding?” — the rule

So the K appears when you DECIDE: after you have all the distances sorted, you choose how many neighbours to consult.

ActivityWhat you doWhere K appears
8.1Compare radar shapes visually❌ No K yet — building intuition
8.2Calculate distances to ALL characters❌ No K yet — getting numbers
8.3Choose K=3, 5, or 7 and count neighbours✅ K appears here!
📌 Quick example — Dog Lock from Activity 8.2:
After calculating all distances and sorting them:
  • If K=3: Look at the 3 closest → 2 Attack, 1 Defense → Attack
  • If K=5: Look at the 5 closest → 3 Attack, 2 Defense → Attack
  • If K=7: Look at the 7 closest → 4 Attack, 3 Defense → Attack
🎯 The K is the number you choose to look at!
🔑 Key takeaway: The radar graph and distance calculations are the preparation. The K is the decision rule you apply after you have the distances.

⚖️ Choosing the right K trade‑offs

  • Small K (e.g., 1 or 3): more sensitive to noise, can overfit.
  • Large K (e.g., 7 or 9): smoother decision boundary, but may underfit.
  • Best practice: test several K values on a validation set and pick the one with highest accuracy.
💡 In Activity 8.3 you compared K = 3, 5, and 7 — which one gave the best accuracy for the wildfire data?
📌 For example: if K=1, the prediction would be based on just the single closest day, which might be an outlier. If K=7, the prediction is based on a larger group, which smooths out unusual data points.
